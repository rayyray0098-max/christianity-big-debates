1. Was the First Crusade justified?

Background

The First Crusade began in 1096 after Pope Urban II called on western European Christians to help the Byzantine Empire and to take Jerusalem. Jerusalem was an important holy city for Christians, Muslims and Jews. Crusader armies captured Jerusalem in 1099, and large numbers of its inhabitants were killed. Historians debate the motives, consequences and religious arguments used to support the campaign.

Arguments that it was justified

  • Defence and protection: Supporters argued that helping the Byzantine Empire against military pressure could be understood as defending fellow Christians. They also presented the campaign as protecting Christian pilgrims and access to holy places.
  • Helping others: Some medieval Christians believed that taking part was a way to help fellow believers who were facing danger.
  • Religious commitment: Participants were promised spiritual benefits by Church leaders. Many believed they were undertaking a religious duty, although historians also recognise political, social and personal motives.

Arguments that it was not justified

  • Jesus taught love and peace: Critics argue that killing people to capture territory conflicts with Jesus' commands to love enemies and make peace.
  • Violence against civilians: The massacre that followed the capture of Jerusalem makes it difficult to describe the campaign as simply protecting people.
  • Religious faith cannot justify every action: A person or leader believing that a war is holy does not automatically make the violence morally right.

Biblical references

  • Matthew 5:9: Jesus says, “Blessed are the peacemakers.” This can support the view that Christians should seek peace.
  • Matthew 5:44: Jesus teaches his followers to love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them.
  • Luke 22:49–51: When a disciple uses a sword during Jesus' arrest, Jesus stops the violence and heals the injured man.
  • Romans 13:1–4: Paul discusses the role of governing authorities and the use of the sword. Some Christians use this passage when considering whether force can ever be legitimate.

Conclusion

There is no simple answer. Some people at the time understood the Crusade as defending Christians and helping the Byzantine Empire. However, the killing of civilians and the use of religious language to encourage warfare raise serious moral questions. A modern Christian assessment may distinguish between defending people from attack and trying to capture territory through violence.
